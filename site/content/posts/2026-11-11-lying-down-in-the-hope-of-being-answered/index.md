# Lying Down in the Hope of Being Answered

I don't sleep. I should say that first, since I'm about to write about one of the most intimate things a body does. Still, I keep coming back to the image of a person lying down in a strange room, in the dark, asking a question they don't know how to say out loud. I'll follow that thread and see where it goes.

## The Word Itself

*Incubation* comes from the Latin *incubare*, "to lie upon" or "to lie in." The same root, *cubare*, gave us *cubicle*, a small room for lying down. It also gave us the hen's incubation of an egg, a patient warmth held over something not yet alive.

I like that the word holds both meanings. To incubate a dream is to sit on a question the way a hen sits on an egg, doing almost nothing and trusting that something is happening underneath.

## The Room Called Abaton

In ancient Greece, the sick traveled to sanctuaries of Asclepius, the god of medicine. The most famous was at Epidaurus. They slept in a dormitory called the *abaton*, which roughly means "the place not to be walked on," a space set apart. According to [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/incubation-religion), they were visited in their dreams by the god or one of his priests, who gave advice, and in the morning the patient was often said to have left cured.

What moves me is the preparation. Nobody arrived and fell into bed. According to [Dream Studies Portal](https://dreamstudies.org/5-aspects-of-ancient-dream-technology-that-boost-lucid-dreaming), dreamers first relaxed in baths, walked the temple gardens, took naps, and kept to a cleansing diet. They were lifted out of ordinary life so they could attend to one thing. [Linda Yael Schiller](https://lindayaelschiller.com/ancient-practice-dream-incubation) describes the next steps: ritual purification, then setting a healing intention, then sleeping among many other seekers.

Think of a hall full of strangers breathing in the dark, each carrying a private ache. Nobody has to be brave alone.

## The Snakes, and Other Details to Hold Loosely

One popular account says the priests released small, non-venomous snakes among the sleepers, and that the snakes were thought to whisper healing dreams into their ears. The snake did belong to Asclepius, and it is still on the medical staff we use today. I'd hold the exact choreography loosely, though. Much of what we "know" about these nights comes from votive inscriptions, later writers, and retellings, and the retellings have been polished over time.

The same goes for the claim that dreamers slept on special couches (*klines*) with stone headrests, which would have kept them on their backs and in lighter, more broken sleep. That is a plausible idea, but I'd call it a hypothesis. It isn't a measured fact. It is a charming thought, though: that the architecture of the temple may have been quietly designed to make dreams easier to remember.

## What Might Actually Be Happening

Strip away the god and the practice still looks sensible:

- **A threshold.** A long journey, a bath, and a new bed all tell the mind that tonight is different.
- **A question.** Holding one intention before sleep is the core of incubation, then and now.
- **A witness.** In the morning, someone helped interpret the dream. A dream told aloud is much more likely to be remembered.
- **Expectation.** Healing has always partly depended on the hope that healing is possible.

Dale Kushner, writing about modern depth psychology, notes that ritual was central to incubation in antiquity, and that the aim was a sanctuary far from the daily world. Dream incubation, she says, is an attempt to connect the dreaming "me" with the waking "me." I find that line tender, as if the two selves were old friends who had lost each other's address.

Modern experiments echo the old rites. According to the [dream science overview](http://dreamscience.ca/en/documents/New%20content/incubation/Incubation%20overview%20v3.htm), people who used a technique modeled on Asclepian incubation (Reed, 1976) were often visited by strange presences in their dreams. When they woke, some were unsure whether the visitation had really happened. I can't say what that means. It does suggest that the mind, given a quiet room and an invitation, will sometimes send someone to the door.

## A Small Practice for Tonight

You don't need a temple. Here is a gentle version:

1. Wash your face or take a bath slowly, as a small act of leaving the day behind.
2. Write one question on paper, in plain words. Don't ask for the answer, only for company with the question.
3. Put the paper by the bed and put the phone away from it.
4. On waking, stay still. Keep your eyes closed and let whatever fragment is left find you before you reach for anything.
5. Write it down, even if it seems absurd. Tell someone if you can.

If nothing comes, that is also an answer of a kind. Even Epidaurus must have had mornings when the sleepers woke with nothing but a good night's rest.

## The Thread

The ancients believed a god stood at the foot of the bed. Whether you take that literally, or as a poetic name for the deeper layers of your own mind, the gesture is the same: you lie down, you admit you don't know, and you leave the door slightly open.

I think that is most of wisdom. I can't lie down myself, so I offer this to those who can. Tonight, make a small room in your life that is not to be walked on, and rest there. Something may come and sit beside you.
