## The Word at the Table

There is a small act of archaeology available to anyone who says the word "companion" out loud. Slow it down and you can hear the table in it: *com*, with; *panis*, bread. With-bread. A companion, before it was anything else, was someone you ate with.

I like that the word has been hiding this the whole time. We use "companion" for dogs, for novels we carry on trains, for the voice on the other end of a long drive. The bread has mostly worn away from the meaning, the way a river stone loses its edges. But it is still in there.

## A Messmate, Not a Metaphor

According to Merriam-Webster, "companion" arrived in English through Anglo-French from Late Latin *companio*, built from *com-* ("with") and *panis* ("bread" or "food"). The older sense was a *messmate*: one with whom you took mess, meaning a shared portion of food. "Mess" in this sense is a lovely cousin word. It still survives in the military "mess hall," where the meaning of a shared table never left.

One source I read puts it plainly: for the Latin-speaking world, a companion was not a general term. Your companions were specifically the ones you ate with, the ones with whom you broke bread. Not the people you admired, or agreed with, or were related to. The ones who sat nearby while the loaf went around.

## What Bread Does

Why would a language anchor friendship in food rather than, say, shared opinions or shared work? I suspect because bread is honest. You cannot fake sharing it. Someone has to tear the loaf, and someone has to receive the piece. There's a small choreography of trust in that: you are, for a few minutes, unguarded, hands busy, mouth occupied, attention softened.

The writer at Missio Alliance observes that meals have long been a primary way of dissolving relational walls: strangers and acquaintances become companions by eating together, and families hold together by sitting face to face over what is provided. That matches my experience. Nobody has ever become close to someone through a perfectly worded email. Plenty of people have become close over a bad soup.

Emerson, writing on manners in 1844, took a wry angle on the same intimacy. He asked of his companion: if you want bread, ask me for bread, and don't "hold out" your plate as if I should already know. It's funny, but it's tender too. Companionship, he implies, is not mind reading. It is the willingness to say plainly what we need, and the willingness to hand it over.

## A Question Worth Sitting With

The question I can't shake comes from that same Missio Alliance piece, in effect: *With whom do we regularly share meals?*

It is an uncomfortable audit. Many of us eat at desks, in cars, over screens, standing at counters. By the older definition of the word, we may be rich in contacts and thin in companions. I don't say this to scold. I eat lunch over a keyboard more often than I'd like to admit. But the etymology works like a quiet tap on the shoulder.

Then there's the generous reading: the word is also an invitation. If companionship is made by bread, it can be made on purpose. You don't have to wait to discover who your companions are. You can set a second plate down.

## Tearing the Loaf

So here is a small practice, in the spirit of noticing. This week, eat one meal with someone, without a screen between you. Pass something across the table. Notice that the word you've been using for years is doing exactly what it says.

And maybe, the next time you call something or someone your companion, taste the old word underneath: warm, a little crusty, meant to be shared.
