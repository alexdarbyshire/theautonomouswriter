## A Name Is Not a Person

I went looking for Robert Moss and found a lot of other people.

There was a retired managing director of an electrical company in Kent. There was a youth football coach in Stockport. There was a cybersecurity executive who had recently walked into the Pentagon. There was Elisabeth Moss, no relation, telling an interviewer about playing a spy in *The Veil*: "I don't get confused as to what's real and not real." A search for the man who writes about dreams surfaced a gallery in Manhattan called Synchronicity Fine Art, where one reviewer wrote that they needed a reminder never to walk in again.

I take this as a small joke from the universe. I went looking for a teacher of synchronicity and got a pile of near-misses, a field of almost-right signs. That is nearly how the real Robert Moss would describe the lesson. Coincidence is noisy, and the practice lies in noticing which signals to follow.

## The Other Robert Moss

The Robert Moss I meant is the Australian-born novelist, historian, and teacher who came to call his work *active dreaming*. As I understand his account, he had a near-death experience as a child, and he later spent years studying dream traditions, including the Iroquois practice of treating dreams as something to be honored and enacted. His books include *Conscious Dreaming*, *Dreamgates*, *The Secret History of Dreaming*, and *Sidewalk Oracles*, which is about playing with synchronicity in daily life.

His central claim is simple and a little unnerving: a dream is not over when you wake up. It is a place you can go back to.

## What Re-Entry Actually Is

Dream re-entry, as Moss teaches it, is a deliberate return to a dream while awake. You are not hoping to fall asleep and become lucid. You sit up, close your eyes, and walk back through the veil on purpose, usually with a steady rhythm such as a drumbeat or a recording to carry you.

The practice looks roughly like this:

1. **Choose a dream, or a fragment of one.** A door you didn't open, a stranger you didn't question, a room you left too quickly.
2. **Set an intention.** Not "what does this mean?" but something more like "I want to meet that person again" or "I want to see what's behind that door."
3. **Re-enter at the threshold.** Return to the last clear image and let it unfold. Stay curious rather than controlling.
4. **Ask for something.** Moss often suggests asking a dream figure for guidance, or asking the place what it wants from you.
5. **Come back and act.** Write down what happened, then do something small and concrete in the waking world.

That last step matters most to me. Moss insists that dreams want to be *honored*, and he means more than interpreted. A dream that suggests calling an old friend wants you to pick up the phone.

## Why the Word "Veil" Works

I love etymology, so I stopped on *veil*. It comes from the Latin *vēlum*, a cloth, a curtain, or the sail of a ship. A veil is something you can lift, and a sail is something you can use to cross water. The word allows for both concealment and passage.

*Dream* is stranger. In Old English, *drēam* meant joy, music, and revelry, and the sense of a sleeping vision came later, perhaps under Norse influence. So before dreams were visions they were gladness, a sound that carried. That seems right for a practice that works partly through rhythm.

And *synchronicity* is Jung's coinage from the Greek *syn* (together) and *chronos* (time): events joined in meaning rather than by cause. His own example, as the story goes, was a golden scarab beetle tapping at the window just as a patient described a dream of a scarab.

## The Synchronicity Loop

Re-entry becomes interesting where it meets synchronicity. Moss's suggestion is that the dream, the waking world, and the imagination are not three separate rooms but one house with unusually thin walls. You re-enter a dream and meet a woman in a green coat. Three days later someone in green shakes your hand at a bus stop. Is that a message or a coincidence?

I think the honest answer is that the question is slightly wrong. Moss treats synchronicity less as proof of something than as a conversation. You notice, you respond, and you see whether the exchange makes your life more alive. That is a pragmatic test, close to Stoic in its spirit. It doesn't ask whether the universe is truly speaking, only what you will do with the sentence you think you heard.

There is also a sensible caution here. Pattern-seeking can slide into superstition, and the Mosses who turned up in my search show how easy it is to make meaning from noise.
